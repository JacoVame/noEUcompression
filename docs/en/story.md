# The story

← [Index](./README.md)

There is a very old, somewhat poetic idea: a single word, on its own, means almost nothing. A word takes meaning from the words around it. "Rain" alone is just a sound, but if "window" sits next to it, it becomes a rain you watch through glass; if "heartbeat" sits next to it, it becomes a rain you listen to. Words are like people in a small town: they get to know each other through the streets they walk, the houses they enter, the hours they keep. If two people walk the same routes, they begin to resemble each other in their manners, even when they never speak directly.

From this idea, simple as a proverb, the prototype you are looking at is born. It is named, half-jokingly, *non-EU*: not because it has anything to do with European regulations, but because it lives in a non-Euclidean geometry. The geometry we are taught at school, the one of rulers and protractors, takes for granted that space is flat. That works wonderfully for measuring a field or designing a building. But language is not a field: it is a tree. A tree where "animal" is a parent, "mammal" a child, "dog" a grandchild, and so on, every level more crowded than the last. To lay such a tree on a flat sheet of paper, you quickly run out of room. It is a bit like trying to fit the genealogy of a vast family on a school notebook: by the end, the names step on each other's toes.

So someone, long ago, asked: what if we changed the geometry? There is a way to picture a space in which the available room grows suddenly as you move away from the centre — not polynomially, not little by little, but exponentially, the way grandchildren multiply along a family tree. That space is called *hyperbolic*, and its most beloved model looks like an Escher dream: a circle in which fish swimming toward the boundary appear to shrink to our eyes, but in truth are all the same size, because near the boundary there is infinitely more room. The model is called the *Poincaré disk*, after the mathematician who studied it. It is small and infinite at the same time: from outside it sits inside a circle; from inside it has no edge.

The prototype takes the text it is given — a paragraph, an article, a chapter — and turns it into a small town. First it cleans the text: drops punctuation, digits, lone letters, unifies accents. Words remain. Then it watches who appears next to whom: it slides a small window across the text, and every time two words sit inside the window together, it notes their meeting. A kind of register of coincidences forms. The word "rain" meets "window", "heartbeat", "light", "calendar". The word "legs" meets "thin", "trembled", "uncertain". Each word, at the end of this census, has its own short list of friends: its *context*.

At this point the system does something that, told this way, sounds like magic but is just arithmetic. It turns the register of coincidences into coordinates. It reduces the long lists into a few digits per word — two, three, four — just enough to draw each word as a point in a space. This is the moment when the text, once a river of words, becomes a handful of stars. The simplest and most robust way to do this transformation is curiously random: pick a few columns of the register at random, sum them, normalise. The result is not beautiful, but it is honest and reproducible. There are more sophisticated ways, but they rarely matter for getting the gist.

Then the interesting part. The stars are not left in flat space — they would step on each other's toes, remember? — but are gently pushed inside the Poincaré disk. The system rescales them radially, brings them all inside a circle of radius one, and changes the ruler: the distance between two words is no longer the Pythagorean one but a formula that accounts for the curvature of the space. It is like switching from a road map to a globe: the same cities, but the distances change, and they change predictably. Common words — articles, prepositions, auxiliaries — settle near the centre. Rare and topical words — those that make a text unique — drift toward the boundary. Near the boundary, remember, space dilates: a small Euclidean gap turns into an enormous hyperbolic one. It is a zoom effect, and it has a definite value: it makes it easy to separate words that truly belong to different families.

Once distances are measured, only one question remains: how close must two words be before we say they are kin? You would need a threshold. Picking it by hand is thankless, because every text has its own scale. The prototype does not pick by hand: it draws a sample of distances at random, studies the distribution, and decides on its own. Three strategies are available, one simple and two slightly more sophisticated, and a fourth that mixes them so as to be robust on regular data and on outlier-heavy data alike. Along with the threshold, the system returns a small dashboard: the median of the distances, a measure of their spread, the histogram. It is its way of saying: *look, this is what I chose, and this is why*.

At this point we build the graph. Two words are linked when their distance falls below the threshold, unlinked otherwise. The graph is traversed with an elegant, ancient tool, *union-find*, which has the virtue of being order-invariant: it does not matter which word you start with, the result will be the same. The connected components of the graph are the *clusters*. Each cluster is a small family of words that, in this text, share a similar context.

On the five lines of paragraph we tested — the opening of Kafka's *Metamorphosis* in Italian — three families emerge: a large one, gathering almost everything, because in such a short text most words share the same fog of context; and two tiny ones, of a single word each, that the system was careful enough to keep apart. It is a sparse result, but an honest one: with so few words there is not enough material for finer cuts. On a full book the families would be many, and more nuanced.

The last gesture is the one that gives the project its name: *compression*. The system closes its eyes and asks: if I replaced every word with the name of its family, and kept on the side a small table saying who belongs to whom, would the vocabulary of the text be smaller? By how much? The answer is a number, and it is not to be taken as seriously as the number `gzip` would give you: it is a thermometer, not an official stamp. It measures how well the families have absorbed the redundancy of the vocabulary. On our paragraph it says fifty-four and a half percent. It is a smile, not a victory.

What does this prototype not do? Many things. It does not really compress bytes; it does not make them smaller for sending across a network. It does not understand sentences: it works on words, one by one. It has no memory of lemmas, so it treats "ran" and "running" as different words. It has not read billions of pages like a large language model: it is naive, fragile, sparse. But for that very reason it has value: it is readable in a few hundred lines of code, runs anywhere Node.js runs, is deterministic, and does one thing only. It is a small telescope to look at a beautiful phenomenon: the fact that words, arranged inside a curved space, recognise each other in families. Nothing more, nothing less.

What could one do with it? Many things. Add a coder that genuinely encodes the clusters as symbols and yields a real compressed file. Replace the random projection with a properly trained embedding. Try distant languages, non-Latin alphabets, poetry, code, music. Validate it on big corpora, compare it against other systems. Make it fast, distributed, capable of streaming. Each of these is a project of its own, and they deserve to be taken on one at a time, calmly. For now the prototype serves the role every prototype plays: to fall in love with an idea, and to see whether it is worth carrying further.

When you close the animated pipeline and the small yellow dot stops running from one station to the next, what stays with you is a feeling that the words, inside that disk, are not just words. They are people in a small non-Euclidean town, who recognise each other through the streets they walk. Those who meet every morning at the market begin to resemble each other. Those who only see one another now and then, in a café near the boundary of town, remain distant, unique, irreducible. Compression, in the end, is just a way of calling them by their family name.
